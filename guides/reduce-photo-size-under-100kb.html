How to Reduce a Photo to Under 100 KB or 200 KB

By Utivaro Team · Last updated September 30, 2026

Two settings decide a photo's file size: its pixel dimensions and its quality. For most photos, a long edge of about 1000 px at quality 70–80 lands under 100 KB, and about 1400 px at quality 80 lands under 200 KB. Busy, detailed photos need smaller settings. The Image Resizer lets you change both and shows the new file size before you download.

Why online forms set a size limit

Job portals, visa and exam applications, school systems and government forms often cap uploads at 50 KB, 100 KB or 200 KB. A photo straight from a phone is usually 2–5 MB, twenty to fifty times too big. The form doesn't need that detail; it needs a clear face or a readable document at a modest size.

Many of these forms also ask for fixed dimensions (for example 600 × 600 px) or accept only JPG. Check the form's instructions first. If it names dimensions, use those exactly and adjust only the quality.

What actually changes the file size: our test

We resized two real photos in Chrome using the same browser canvas method the Image Resizer uses, and saved each at four quality levels. Photo A is an indoor shot of two people against a plain wall. Photo B is a busy street scene with a bus, people and signs. Sizes are in KB (1 KB = 1024 bytes).

Three things stand out:

  • Quality is the fastest lever. Going from 92 to 70 roughly halves the file (103 → 48 KB for Photo A, 192 → 101 KB for Photo B) while the dimensions stay the same. On a phone screen, the difference between 92 and 75 is hard to see.
  • Dimensions work on area. Halving both width and height leaves a quarter of the pixels, so the file shrinks sharply. From 600 × 800 to 300 × 400, Photo B dropped from 125 KB to 35 KB at quality 80.
  • Content matters as much as settings. At quality 80, Photo B used about 2.1 bits per pixel and Photo A about 1.0. The busy photo needed twice the space for the same number of pixels. Leaves, gravel, crowds, fabric patterns and camera noise all cost bytes; smooth walls and skies are cheap.

A quick way to estimate

From those numbers you can work out how many pixels fit in a limit. At quality 80, a typical photo takes about 1–2 bits per pixel:

Step by step: get a photo under 100 KB

  1. Start from the original photo, not a screenshot or a copy downloaded from a chat app. If it's an iPhone HEIC file, convert it first with HEIC to JPG.
  2. Open the Image Resizer and choose the photo. Leave Keep aspect ratio on.
  3. Set the width. If the form gives dimensions, enter them. Otherwise start with 1000 px on the long side for a 100 KB limit, or 1400 px for 200 KB.
  4. Choose JPG as the output format unless the form says otherwise, and set quality to 80.
  5. Resize and read the new size in the result panel.
  6. Too big? Lower the quality to 70. Still too big? Reduce the width by about 20% (1000 → 800 px) and try again. Repeat until it fits.
  7. Download and check the photo at full size. If faces or text look blocky, raise the quality and reduce the dimensions instead.

Should you use WebP instead?

WebP files are usually smaller. In our test at quality 80, WebP was 45% smaller than JPG for Photo A (34 vs 63 KB at 1024 × 576) and 12% smaller for Photo B (109 vs 125 KB at 600 × 800). But many upload forms accept only JPG or PNG. Use WebP for your own website, where it speeds up pages; see the JPG to WebP converter. For forms, stick to JPG unless WebP is listed.

Avoid PNG for photos. PNG is lossless, so a photo saved as PNG is often several times larger than the same photo as JPG, and lowering quality has no effect on it.

Common problems

  • The form still says the file is too large. Some forms count 1 KB as 1000 bytes, not 1024. Aim for 90 KB when the limit is 100 KB, and 180 KB when it is 200 KB.
  • The form rejects the file even though it's small. Check the format (JPG vs JPEG is the same thing, but HEIC and WebP often aren't accepted) and the required dimensions.
  • The photo looks blurry. The dimensions are probably too small. Use more pixels at a lower quality instead; a 900 px photo at quality 70 usually looks better than a 500 px photo at quality 92.
  • Colors look banded in the sky or on walls. Quality is too low for smooth areas. Go back up to 75–80 and reduce the dimensions slightly.
  • A document scan is unreadable. Text needs sharp edges. Keep at least 1000 px on the long side, crop away empty margins first, and lower quality only to 75.

Frequently asked questions

What dimensions should a 100 KB photo be?

Around 1000 px on the long side at quality 70–80 for most photos, and 700–800 px for very detailed ones. If the form specifies dimensions, use those and adjust quality only.

Does reducing the file size reduce quality?

Some detail is always removed, but at quality 70–80 the loss is hard to see on a screen. Heavy compression (below 60) starts to show blocks around edges.

Can I compress a photo without changing its dimensions?

Yes. Enter the original width and height in the Image Resizer, choose JPG and lower the quality. This works when the photo is only slightly over the limit.

Is my photo uploaded when I resize it?

No. The Image Resizer processes the photo in your browser, so ID photos and documents stay on your device.
